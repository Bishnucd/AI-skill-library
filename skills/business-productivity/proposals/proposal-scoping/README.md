# proposal-scoping

## What it does

Converts a vague client request into a scoped proposal: verifiable deliverables, explicit
exclusions, assumptions with stated consequences, a dependency-gated timeline, a price basis, and
change-order triggers.

## Why it exists

Projects lose money at the edges. "A new website" quietly includes content migration, image
licensing, and a month of support, none of which was priced. Writing the exclusions and the
assumptions — and what happens if an assumption fails — turns those edges into a documented
agreement instead of an argument.

## When to use

- Writing a proposal, quote, or statement of work.
- Rescoping a project that is over budget.
- Comparing two requests that sound similar but differ in effort.
- Converting a discovery call into a fixed-scope engagement.

## When not to use

- Employment contracts or legal agreements, which need legal review.
- Internal estimates for a single task.
- Ongoing retainer renewals with unchanged scope.

## Requirements

- The client request, ideally in their words.
- Your rates, capacity, and any third-party costs.
- The client's decision timeline and constraints.

## How to use

1. Paste the request and any call notes.
2. The agent separates said, implied, and unsaid expectations.
3. It writes deliverables with completion tests, then exclusions.
4. It states assumptions and their consequences.
5. It produces the timeline, price basis, and change-order process.

## Example

```
Use proposal-scoping on this discovery call transcript for a marketing site.
```

Result: six testable deliverables, nine exclusions, four assumptions with consequences, a
copy-dependent timeline, and a fixed price with three acceptance milestones.

## Limitations

- Cannot determine your actual cost or capacity; it structures, you price.
- Does not provide legal review of contract terms.
- Estimates remain estimates; the skill makes the basis explicit rather than accurate.
- Client negotiation strategy is out of scope.
